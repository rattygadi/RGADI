Published research · Ice–ocean interactions

Grounding-zone melting and seawater intrusion

How kilometer-scale tidal seawater intrusions transport ocean heat beneath grounded ice, and what this means for glacier evolution.

How does an ice grounding zone form?

Satellite double-differential interferometric synthetic aperture radar (DInSAR) observations show that the grounding line can migrate by kilometers over a tidal cycle—not merely by meters as simpler flotation-based expectations might suggest. This migration defines an ice grounding zone (IGZ), where seawater periodically intrudes beneath ice that remains grounded at other stages of the tide. Such kilometer-scale zones have been observed at Petermann Glacier in Greenland and Thwaites Glacier in Antarctica.

Why do thin seawater intrusions matter?

Although the seawater layer beneath grounded ice may be only millimeters to centimeters thick, its horizontal reach can extend over kilometers. Repeated tidal opening and closure drives water exchange through this shallow cavity. The resulting flow can deliver ocean heat to the ice base and sustain basal melting. In this way, a narrow, periodically flooded cavity can have consequences well beyond what its thickness might suggest.

What have we learned from Petermann and Thwaites?

In two-dimensional high-resolution MITgcm studies of Petermann Glacier (Gadi et al., 2023) and Thwaites Glacier (Gadi et al., 2025), we investigated how tidal seawater exchange controls melting within the IGZ. Both studies found elevated melt rates near the entrance of the IGZ cavity, followed by a strong decline farther inland as the intruding seawater loses heat and the flow weakens.

The 2025 Thwaites study characterizes the modeled inland melt-rate decrease as quadratic, rather than strictly linear. The studies also show that melt depends on ocean thermal forcing and the length and geometry of the intrusion cavity. Importantly, representing an IGZ allows warm ocean water to melt ice upstream of the conventional fixed grounding line—where models without seawater intrusion would otherwise impose little or no melting.

Implications for sea-level rise

These results suggest that glaciers with kilometer-scale IGZs can be more sensitive to ocean warming than models that exclude tidal seawater intrusion. Melting beneath grounded ice may reduce basal contact and resistance to flow, influencing grounding-line retreat and ice discharge. Incorporating physically grounded representations of IGZ exchange and melting into large-scale ice-sheet models is therefore important for improving projections of future sea-level rise.
