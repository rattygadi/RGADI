About

Growing up along the coast of Visakhapatnam, India, the ocean was always part of my surroundings. Over the years, I noticed changes along the shoreline, including how much closer the water appeared to reach the seawall compared with my childhood. These experiences helped shape my curiosity about the ocean and, eventually, my interest in understanding how changes in the climate system affect coastlines and the communities that live along them.

My academic journey began in naval architecture and marine engineering, where I developed a foundation in fluid mechanics and the interaction of fluids with moving boundaries. I subsequently studied nonlinear hydrodynamics at TU Delft and worked on ocean modeling at the Indian Institute of Science. During my PhD at UC Irvine, I applied this background to investigate seawater intrusions and basal melting in ice grounding zones, combining numerical simulations with satellite and in situ observations. At Caltech, I have extended this work to three-dimensional ocean circulation beneath ice shelves, tidal flexure, residual overturning, eddy transport, and water-mass transformation.

My research seeks to understand the physical processes that shape ice-shelf morphology and govern its evolution through interactions between ice dynamics and ocean circulation. Beyond identifying these mechanisms, my long-term goal is to translate that understanding into physically grounded representations of ice–ocean interactions in large-scale ice-sheet and climate models, ultimately contributing to more reliable projections of future sea-level rise.

Scientific vision

Research directions

My research examines how ice-shelf morphology, ocean circulation, basal melting, and ice motion influence one another. I combine process-focused numerical models with remote sensing and in situ observations to identify the mechanisms behind these interactions.

Published research

Grounding-zone melting and seawater intrusion

Mechanistic studies of seawater exchange and basal melting in the grounding zones of Petermann Glacier and Thwaites Glacier. Idealized ocean experiments are interpreted alongside satellite-derived melt rates, airborne geometry, and in situ ocean and melt observations.

Ongoing research

Tidal flexure and cavity circulation

Source-level development of MITgcm with time-dependent ice loading and moving ice boundaries to investigate the effects of tidal flexure on high-resolution overturning, ocean pathways, and basal melt distributions.

Ongoing research

Residual transport, eddies, and water-mass transformation

Diagnosing Eulerian and residual overturning, eddy-induced transport, buoyancy transformation, and heat budgets to distinguish dynamical redistribution from irreversible water-mass changes.

Future directions

Observation–model integration

Combining satellite and field observations with numerical simulations to test grounding-zone physics, guide observing strategies, and develop physically constrained representations of ice–ocean coupling.

Methods and expertise

  • Ocean modeling: source-level MITgcm development; two- and three-dimensional cavities; time-dependent ice-load forcing; grounding-line migration and shelf-ice remeshing.
  • Transport diagnostics: residual overturning, water-mass transformation, eddy heat transport, diapycnal transport, heat and volume budgets.
  • Satellite and airborne: ICESat-2 ATL14/15; TanDEM-X DEM time series; Operation IceBridge ATM LiDAR and MCoRDS radar; bending-zone analysis.
  • In situ: ApRES; seismic hammer and Geode processing; ROV observations of ice base and seabed.
  • Programming: MATLAB, Fortran, Python.
